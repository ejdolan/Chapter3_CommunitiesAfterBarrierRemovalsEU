# Chapter3_CommunitiesAfterBarrierRemovalsEU

This is the location where the data and code corresponding to my PhD _**Chapter 3 (Communities Recover After Barrier Removals in European River Basins)**_ are stored.

**Abstract**
River fragmentation is widespread across Europe, and accelerating programmes of barrier removal seek to restore longitudinal connectivity within freshwater networks. However, the ecological effects of existing barrier pressure and subsequent barrier removal remain difficult to generalise beyond individual restoration projects and focal species. Here, we integrated long-term riverine invertebrate community data with European barrier inventories, barrier-removal records, river-network topology, climate and human population pressure to examine community responses to both existing barriers and their removal. Existing-barrier analyses comprised 26,039 site-year observations across 1,772 sites and 397 river sub-basins. Barrier density and barrier type showed no credible association with total taxon richness, Shannon diversity or the proportion of non-native taxa. Instead, community structure was more consistently associated with climatic, anthropogenic and river-network context, including lower richness and diversity with increasing human population density and strong spatial structuring of non-native taxa within river networks. In contrast, post-removal analyses of 3,547 observations from 655 sites showed pronounced temporal dynamics. Richness and Shannon diversity increased non-linearly with time since removal, with stronger increases where pre-removal communities had lower richness or diversity. The proportion of non-native taxa declined through time, particularly at sites with initially high non-native representation, although responses also varied with hydrological distance, network position and intervening barriers. These findings indicate that static measures of barrier burden alone may poorly predict contemporary invertebrate community structure, whereas biological responses following reconnection are strongly dependent on initial community state and river-network context. Incorporating ecological baselines and spatial connectivity into restoration planning may therefore improve predictions of biodiversity responses to river barrier removal.

**Keywords**
Biological invasions; connectivity restoration; freshwater invertebrates; long-term recovery; river barriers; river restoration
